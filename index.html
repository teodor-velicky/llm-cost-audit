Engagement note  ·  LLM cost engineering

Where the LLM bill was going

The team had shipped AI features quickly and they worked. Nobody could say what each one cost, which model it ran on, or whether the bill was growing because of real usage or because of waste.

01

The classifier was paying full price eight times over

Every classification re-sent the same long instructions and re-processed emails that had already been handled. I changed it to a prime-then-fan-out pattern: one call writes the shared prefix into the prompt cache, then the parallel calls read it back at the cached rate instead of paying full input price. I also removed a reprocess=1 flag that was pushing already-classified emails back through the model.

The pipeline's cost dropped by roughly 60%. The outputs did not change.

02

One way of measuring, before changing anything else

I went through every AI automation in the company and built a single measurement: Anthropic invoice data reconciled against per-call token counts times list pricing, broken down per workflow and per model. That gave the team a cost per feature — and it caught a cache-token accounting mismatch that had been inflating the bill.

03

Two changes I recommended against

With numbers in hand I evaluated the two things everyone reaches for first. For this workload the quality loss and the extra latency were not worth the savings. I recommended against both and wrote down the reasoning, so the decision can be reopened rather than re-argued.

Move workflows to a cheaper model Not adopted

The saving was real but small against the measured baseline, and the quality drop on this workload was not acceptable for output that goes out under the company's name.

Revisit when: the price gap between tiers widens, or an eval suite exists that can prove the cheaper model holds on this exact task.

Cascade: cheap model first, strong model on low confidence Not adopted

A cascade only pays if the cheap model handles most traffic confidently. Here the escalation rate meant paying for two calls often enough to erase the saving, while adding a second round-trip to every escalated request.

Revisit when: traffic mix shifts toward easy cases, or list prices move enough to change the escalation break-even.

04

Prompts out of the codebase, without silent regressions

Prompts were string literals scattered across the codebase, so changing wording meant a developer, a branch and a deploy. I moved them into a prompt editor with access controls and a byte-parity check: an edited prompt has to produce exactly the bytes the code used to hard-code before it can go live. Product people can change wording without an engineer, and without a silent behaviour change nobody notices for a week.

05

Jobs and costs made visible

The automations all ran out of a single cron.php. I split it into separate jobs and built a UI to edit cron tasks, plus an overview page showing what runs, when, and what it costs. Cost stopped being a monthly surprise and became a column next to the job that caused it.

06

Dead AI code removed

Leftovers from earlier experiments were deleted, so the team has less to reason about the next time someone asks what the AI stack actually does.

What the engagement left behind

  • Roughly 60% lower cost on the highest-volume pipeline, with identical output.
  • Per-feature and per-model cost that reconciles against the actual invoice.
  • Two expensive changes avoided on the strength of measurement, with the reasoning written down.
  • Prompts, jobs and costs editable and visible in a UI instead of buried in code.
07

If you run LLMs in production, the order matters

Most cost conversations start at the last step — which model, which tier — because that is the step with a price list attached. It is the step with the least leverage and the most risk to quality. Structural waste is cheaper to remove and costs nothing in output.

How I work with teams on this

A fixed-price one-week audit: every AI workflow measured against the invoice, structural waste identified and quantified, and a written recommendation on what to change and what to leave alone. An implementation sprint afterwards is optional — the audit stands on its own, and the fixes ship as reviewed pull requests in your codebase, not as a slide deck.
